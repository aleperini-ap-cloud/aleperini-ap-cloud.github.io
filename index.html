Alessandro Perini

PhD student in Industrial Engineering, University of Rome Tor Vergata

Alessandro Perini received a BSc in Mechanical Engineering (2022) at Roma Tre University, and an MSc in Mechatronics Engineering (2024, cum laude) at the University of Rome Tor Vergata. During his MSc, he worked on the design of tendon-driven hand exoskeletons for stroke rehabilitation.

His PhD research focuses on wearable robotics with a textile substrate, integrating active elements such as tendons or smart materials as well as biomechanical sensors, developed in collaboration with Sensor Medica. In 2026, he was a visiting student at the ARIES laboratory at MIRMI, Technical University of Munich, developing a motion assistance device for the lower limbs.

Research

The work centers on wearable robots with a textile substrate: soft exoskeletons that combine active elements, such as tendons or smart materials, with biomechanical sensors. Applications range from hand rehabilitation after stroke to motion assistance for the lower limbs.

Textile hand exoskeleton

TEXO

A lightweight, accessible glove exoskeleton, fully made of sustainable textiles. It is tendon-driven, and its parametric patterns allow each glove to be tailored to the user.

  • Parametric

    The glove is generated from a small set of parameters, so the pattern is a function of the design, not a fixed drawing.

  • Tailored

    Each glove is fitted to the hand of its user by changing the parameters, with no need for a stock of sizes.

  • Open access

    The design is meant to be easy to reproduce, made of low-cost sustainable textiles and accessible fabrication tools.

  • Textiles
  • Tendon-driven
  • Parametric design
  • Open access

Lower-limb exoskeleton

KNEXO

A lightweight (<1.5 kg), back-worn soft knee exoskeleton for continuous, terrain-adaptive assistance, developed during a 2026 visit at the ARIES lab (MIRMI, Technical University of Munich).

KNEXO uses a physics-based control strategy built on direct barometric measurements, with no terrain classification and no data-driven training. A fuzzy-inspired controller fuses kinematic cues from IMUs with altitude: since the barometer measures altitude change directly, the response follows from the physics of the task at a fraction of the computational cost. An indoor study with eight subjects showed a 5.6% reduction in metabolic cost of transport on stairs compared with the exoskeleton off, and an outdoor study with four participants confirmed continuous variable assistance across walking speeds.

  • Knee extension
  • Barometric sensing
  • Terrain-adaptive control
